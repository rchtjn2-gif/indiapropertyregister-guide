# Buying a bank auction property in India: a due-diligence checklist

Banks sell properties of defaulting borrowers through public auctions, often below market price. The discount comes with risks, so check carefully before you bid.

## How SARFAESI auctions work

Under the SARFAESI Act, 2002, a secured lender can take possession of mortgaged property and sell it without going to court first. The bank publishes a sale notice, usually in newspapers and on its website or an e-auction portal, with a reserve price, an earnest money deposit (EMD), inspection dates and the auction date. Most auctions are held online.

## Reading the auction notice

Look for:

- **Reserve price**: the minimum bid.
- **EMD**: the deposit you must pay to bid, usually refundable if you lose.
- **Property description**: survey or plot number, area and address.
- **Type of possession**: symbolic or physical.
- **Known encumbrances or dues** disclosed by the bank.
- **"As is where is" terms**: you take the property with whatever problems it has.

[India Property Register's bank auctions page](https://indiapropertyregister.com/auctions) brings auction notices together so you can see what is on offer.

## Checking land records and encumbrances

- Match the survey, khasra or plot number in the notice with the state land records.
- Get an encumbrance certificate (or the state equivalent) for the period that matters.
- Look for other loans, court cases or attachments on the property.
- For apartments, check RERA registration and society or builder dues.

You can find plots by village and number through the [India Property Registry](https://indiapropertyregister.com/ipregistry).

## Possession, dues and other risks

- **Physical possession**: if the bank has only symbolic possession, getting the occupant out can take time.
- **Unpaid dues**: property tax, electricity, water and society maintenance may fall on the buyer.
- **Borrower challenges**: the borrower may contest the sale before the Debts Recovery Tribunal.

## Getting a title search done

Before bidding on a large property, have an advocate run a title search covering the chain of documents, encumbrances and litigation. An online page is not proof of title.
